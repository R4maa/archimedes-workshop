How to read a snow crystal

The middle froze first and the tips froze last, so read it from the inside out. Dry air grows flat, faceted plates. Wet air near −15 °C grows branches, and the wetter it is the more fern-like they get. Between −4 and −10 °C the crystal grows into a column, which seen from above barely widens: a small, sharp hexagon. Ribs and bright edges mark where the weather changed.

What is happening here

In 1936 Ukichiro Nakaya grew the first artificial snow crystals on a rabbit hair in a cold room in Sapporo. Changing the temperature and the humidity, he found that each combination grows its own shape. His chart of them, redrawn here, means a crystal can be read backwards: its shape tells you the weather it fell through.

The crystal grows by Gravner and Griffeath’s lattice model (2008). Each site of a hexagonal grid holds vapour, a thin liquid-like layer and ice. Vapour diffuses, the edge freezes some of it, and an edge site joins the crystal once it has gathered enough mass. It needs less when it touches more crystal, which keeps facets flat, while tips that reach wetter air outgrow everything else and branch. The diagram’s colours are the model’s own response at each point.

All six arms grow through the same sky, so they come out identical. Real snowflakes are close to that, but not exact. The page draws the ice by refracting a dark background through its thickness, as in dark-field photographs of real snow.
